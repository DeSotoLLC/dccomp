# A Response to Torah-Keeping / Judaizing Theology

## 1. The Distinction Between the Tablets and the Book of the Law

Deuteronomy 31:26 places the "Book of the Law" written by Moses *beside* the ark of the covenant — not inside it — to function as a witness against Israel:

> "Take this Book of the Law and place it beside the ark of the covenant of the LORD your God, that it may be there for a witness against you." (Deut 31:26)

By contrast, only the tablets of the Testimony — the Ten Commandments inscribed by the finger of God — were placed *inside* the ark:

- **Exodus 40:20** — "He took the testimony and put it into the ark."
- **1 Kings 8:9** — "There was nothing in the ark except the two tablets of stone that Moses put there at Horeb."
- **Hebrews 9:4** — names the ark's contents as the tablets of the covenant.

The spatial distinction is not incidental. It signals a categorical difference between the unchanging moral law written by God Himself and the broader ceremonial code Moses recorded — the latter functioning as a temporary witness to Israel's failure under that system.

## 2. Hebrews on the Ceremonial System

Hebrews argues systematically that the sacrificial system, the Levitical priesthood, the tabernacle, and the feasts tied to them were "a shadow of the good things to come, instead of the true form of these realities" (Heb 10:1).

> "When he said above, 'You have neither desired nor taken pleasure in sacrifices and offerings and burnt offerings and sin offerings' (these are offered according to the law), then he added, 'Behold, I have come to do your will.' He does away with the first in order to establish the second." (Heb 10:8–9)

The author could not be clearer: the ceremonial *first* is done away with so the *second* can be established. Paul says the same thing in Colossians: festivals, new moons, and Sabbaths "are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ" (Col 2:16–17).

## 3. The Inconsistency of Selective Torah-Keeping

If someone insists Christians must keep the feasts but concedes that the sacrifices are no longer required "because we have Jesus," they have done two things at once:

1. **Affirmed the principle of fulfillment** — that Christ's work supersedes part of the Mosaic system.
2. **Invented a man-made tradition** — choosing which parts of an integrated system to keep and which to drop, a division Scripture nowhere authorizes.

The feasts were *temple feasts*. Passover required a lamb sacrificed at the central sanctuary (Deut 16:5–6). The Day of Atonement required the high priest entering the Most Holy Place with sacrificial blood. Pentecost, Trumpets, and Tabernacles were inseparable from priesthood, altar, and pilgrimage. James warns, "Whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has become accountable for all of it" (James 2:10). One cannot dismantle the system and then claim faithfulness to fragments of it.

## 4. Morality Before Sinai; Covenant At Sinai

Moral norms predate Sinai. Cain knew murder was wrong (Gen 4). Joseph called adultery sin against God before any law was given through Moses (Gen 39:9). The Sabbath principle is grounded in creation (Gen 2:2–3). But the *Mosaic covenant* — its priesthood, feasts, dietary laws, and sacrificial structure — began with Moses and was given to one nation as a tutor "until Christ came" (Gal 3:24).

Paul's argument in Romans 4 is decisive: Abraham was justified by faith *before* circumcision, *before* Sinai, *before* the giving of the law. Therefore the promise rests on faith,

> "in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring — not only to the adherent of the law but also to the one who shares the faith of Abraham." (Rom 4:16)

The whole point of locating the promise *before* Sinai is so that righteousness is by faith and Gentiles can be grafted in (Rom 11) without becoming Jews.

## 5. The Calendar Problem

A practical problem compounds the theological one. The original biblical calendar was anchored to temple observation, priestly oversight, and the Sanhedrin's intercalation. None of that has been operative since the temple was destroyed in AD 70.

What modern Torah-keeping movements call "the Hebrew calendar" is itself a rabbinic computation finalized in the medieval period — a tradition of men by their own standard. Other movements use an Enoch calendar, a Zadok / Qumran calendar, lunar-Sabbath schemes, aviv-barley sightings, or equinox-anchored reckonings — and they do not agree with one another. Adherents argue endlessly over which "appointed times" are correct because there is no apostolic authority to settle the matter — and there is no such authority because the New Testament never commands Gentile believers to keep them in the first place.

> "Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ." (Col 2:16–17)

## 6. The Heart of the Issue: Faith Plus Nothing

The gospel is that salvation rests on the finished work of Christ, received by faith alone. Paul wrote Galatians precisely because Judaizers were adding law-keeping to faith:

> "I do not nullify the grace of God, for if righteousness were through the law, then Christ died for no purpose." (Gal 2:21)

> "You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by the law; you have fallen away from grace." (Gal 5:4)

The Jerusalem Council settled the question for the Gentiles directly (Acts 15): they were not to be burdened with the yoke of the law. To bind Christians to the feasts, to dietary law, or to ceremonial Sabbath observance as a condition of faithfulness is to add to the gospel — and Paul's response to that is severe:

> "But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed." (Gal 1:8)

That is not pious zeal. It is a different gospel.

## 7. Conclusion

Scripture distinguishes between the moral law placed *inside* the ark and the ceremonial code placed *beside* it. Hebrews declares the ceremonial system fulfilled and set aside in Christ. The feasts cannot be detached from the temple, the priesthood, and the sacrifices that gave them meaning; selective Torah-keeping is a tradition of men, not the teaching of the apostles. The promise to Abraham rested on faith before Sinai precisely so it could extend to all nations through Christ. To require believers today to observe feasts, calendars, and ceremonial obligations — especially when no one can even agree which calendar to use — is to yoke them to a shadow of a system that God Himself has fulfilled in His Son.

> "For freedom Christ has set us free; stand firm therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery." (Gal 5:1)

# Part II — Response to the "Father Speaking to His Children" Reframing

A common Torah-keeping rebuttal goes like this: *"You're arguing law. I'm talking about a Father speaking to His children. Torah doesn't mean law — the root is yarah, 'to point' or 'to direct.' It's a Father's loving instruction, not a legal code. Asking 'which parts still apply' is Roman law thinking, not Hebraic."*

It sounds spiritual. It is not actually responsive. Here is why.

## 8. The Etymological Fallacy

"Torah means instruction, not law" is a textbook etymological fallacy. Word meaning is determined by usage, not by the supposed root. By the standards of usage:

- The Jewish translators of the Septuagint (3rd–2nd century BC, before any "Roman influence") rendered *torah* as *nomos* — law.
- Yeshua Himself referred to it as "the Law" (Matt 5:17; Luke 24:44).
- Paul, a Pharisee trained at the feet of Gamaliel — about as Hebraic a pedigree as exists — used *nomos* throughout Romans and Galatians and made precisely the distinctions Torah-keepers reject.
- James, brother of the Lord, calls it *nomos* (James 2:8–10).

If "Torah doesn't mean law," someone needs to inform the apostles, the Septuagint translators, and the Lord Himself.

## 9. The Reframe Doesn't Escape the Question

Even granting that Torah is fatherly instruction, the question is unchanged: **which instructions are normative for Gentile believers today?**

The Torah-keeper does not stone a rebellious son (Deut 21:18–21). Does not execute Sabbath-breakers (Ex 31:14). Does not bring a sin offering after childbirth (Lev 12). Does not enforce Levirate marriage (Deut 25). Does not segregate menstruating women (Lev 15). Does not bring tithes to Levitical priests in Jerusalem.

He is *already* filtering. He is *already* deciding which "fatherly instructions" still bind. The only question is whether he will admit it. Calling the filtering process "relationship" instead of "adjudication" doesn't make the filtering go away — it just hides it under sentimental vocabulary. *That* is the actual sleight of hand.

## 10. "If You Love Me, Keep My Commandments"

John 14:15 is constantly cited as if it self-evidently means "keep the Mosaic ceremonial code." It does not. Yeshua said *MY* commandments. In the same discourse He gives the defining one: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another" (John 13:34). The apostle who recorded those words tells us plainly what they mean:

> "For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments. **And his commandments are not burdensome.**" (1 John 5:3)

Compare that with Peter's verdict on the Mosaic yoke at the Jerusalem Council:

> "Why are you putting God to the test by placing a yoke on the neck of the disciples that **neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear**?" (Acts 15:10)

The "commandments" of Christ that are "not burdensome" cannot be the Mosaic yoke that Peter said *no one* — including the patriarchs — could bear. The categories are distinguished by the apostles themselves.

## 11. Redemption Before Commandment Cuts the Other Way

Yes — Exodus 20 begins with "I am HaShem your God who brought you out of Egypt." Redemption precedes commandment. That is exactly the New Covenant pattern, and it is exactly why Mosaic ceremonial obligations don't bind Gentiles in Christ. Israel was redeemed nationally from Egypt and *then* given Sinai as her national covenant. Believers are redeemed in Christ and *then* given His commandments — written on the heart (Jer 31:31–34; Heb 8:8–13), not chiseled on stone for one nation.

The New Covenant is explicitly described as "not like the covenant that I made with their fathers" (Jer 31:32). That is Jeremiah, not Augustine.

## 12. The "Hebraic vs. Roman/Greek Thinking" Trope

The clean dichotomy between "Hebraic relational thinking" and "Greco-Roman legal thinking" is itself a 20th-century academic construct (Boman, Cullmann, et al.) that biblical scholarship has thoroughly dismantled (see James Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961). It is not how the apostles thought. It is not how the rabbis thought. The Mishnah and Talmud — the most Hebraic literature in existence — are densely *legal* texts that adjudicate categories of commandment relentlessly. The very tradition Torah-keepers borrow from spends thousands of pages doing what they claim is "Roman thinking."

The distinction between moral, ceremonial, and judicial law is not Augustine's invention. It is found in Jewish sources before him and is operative in the apostolic writings — Acts 15, Galatians, Colossians 2, the entire book of Hebrews. To wave the whole apostolic testimony off as "Augustinian" is to admit you are in conflict with the New Testament itself.

## 13. Hebrews Is the Apostolic Answer to This Reframing

The book of Hebrews is, from beginning to end, written by a Jew, to Jews, in Jewish categories, demonstrating from the Hebrew Scriptures that the priesthood, sacrificial system, and ceremonial worship of Sinai have been fulfilled and superseded in Messiah:

> "In speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete. And what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away." (Heb 8:13)

That is the apostolic verdict, in the most Hebraic book in the New Testament. No Roman influence required.

## 14. Sonship Does Not Erase the Distinction; It Establishes It

The "sonship vs. legalism" framing actually proves the point. A son is not under a tutor (Gal 3:24–4:7). Paul uses precisely this father-and-children imagery — and his conclusion is the *opposite* of the Torah-keeper's:

> "So with us; while we were children, we were enslaved to the elementary principles of the world. But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son... so that we might receive adoption as sons... So you are no longer a slave, but a son." (Gal 4:3–7)

> "Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods. But now that you have come to know God... how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles of the world, whose slaves you want to be once more? **You observe days and months and seasons and years!** I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain." (Gal 4:8–11)

Paul names the symptom of the false sonship explicitly: observing days, months, seasons, and years. That is not "the Father's loving instruction." Paul calls it returning to slavery — and calls his labor over such people possibly wasted.

## 15. The Real Issue

The Torah-keeper's reframing is not a refutation of the original argument. It is a substitute for one. It changes the vocabulary from "law" to "fatherly instruction," from "obedience" to "relationship," from "commandment" to "sonship" — but never actually answers the question that has been on the table since Acts 15:

**Are Gentile believers obligated to keep the Mosaic ceremonial code?**

The apostles answered: *no.* They answered it as Jews, in Hebraic categories, on the authority of the Spirit (Acts 15:28). And the next time someone tried to override that answer in Galatia, Paul called it a different gospel and pronounced anathema on it.

That is not Augustine. That is not Aquinas. That is the New Testament. Any framework that has to dismiss the apostles as Roman-influenced legalists in order to survive has already conceded the argument.
