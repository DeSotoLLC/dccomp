# Reply to Sergio

Sergio,

You haven't refuted the argument. You've changed the vocabulary and called the change a refutation. Let's slow down.

**1. "Torah doesn't mean law."**
That is the etymological fallacy. Words don't mean what their roots once gestured toward; they mean what they're used to mean. By usage:

- The *Jewish* translators of the Septuagint — 250 years before any Roman influence — rendered *torah* as *nomos*. Law.
- Yeshua called it "the Law" (Matt 5:17; Luke 24:44).
- Paul, a Pharisee trained at the feet of Gamaliel — about as Hebraic as a man can be — called it *nomos* and built whole letters around the distinctions you say don't exist.
- James, brother of the Lord, calls it *nomos* (James 2:8–10).

If "Torah doesn't mean law," someone needs to inform Yeshua, Paul, James, and the Septuagint. The "yarah-means-arrow" move is a youth-pastor word study, not an argument.

**2. The reframe doesn't escape the question; it hides it.**
You say asking "which parts still apply" is Roman law thinking. Fine. Then answer plainly: do *you* stone a rebellious son (Deut 21:18–21)? Execute Sabbath-breakers (Ex 31:14)? Bring a sin offering after childbirth (Lev 12)? Practice Levirate marriage (Deut 25)? Segregate menstruating women (Lev 15)? Tithe to a Levitical priesthood in Jerusalem?

You already filter. You filter constantly. You just refuse to *name* the filtering, because naming it would force you to do exactly what you're accusing me of: adjudicate. You've smuggled the entire ceremonial/judicial/moral distinction into your own practice and then declared it Roman when someone else articulates it. That isn't sonship. It's sleight of hand with a Hebrew accent.

**3. "If you love me, you will keep my commandments."**
Yeshua said *MY* commandments. In the same discourse he tells you which: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another" (John 13:34). And the apostle who recorded those words tells you what they mean:

> "For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments. **And his commandments are not burdensome.**" (1 John 5:3)

Now line that up with Peter, the Jewish apostle, at the Jerusalem Council:

> "Why are you putting God to the test by placing a yoke on the neck of the disciples that **neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear**?" (Acts 15:10)

If the commandments of Christ are "not burdensome," and the Mosaic yoke is one no one — *not even the patriarchs* — could bear, you cannot collapse the two without contradicting both John and Peter. They are different categories. The apostles said so.

**4. Redemption-before-commandment cuts the other way.**
Yes — Exodus 20 begins with redemption from Egypt, then the commandments follow. That is exactly the New Covenant pattern, and it is exactly why the Mosaic ceremonial code does not bind Gentiles in Messiah. Israel was redeemed nationally and *then* given Sinai as her national covenant. Believers are redeemed in Christ and *then* given His commandments — written on the heart, not chiseled on stone for one nation:

> "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant... **not like the covenant that I made with their fathers** on the day when I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt." (Jer 31:31–32)

That is Jeremiah. Not Augustine.

**5. The "Hebraic vs. Roman thinking" trope is a modern academic myth.**
The clean dichotomy you're leaning on — relational Hebrew vs. legalistic Roman — is a 20th-century construct (Boman, Cullmann) that biblical scholarship dismantled decades ago (James Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961). The most Hebraic literature on earth — the Mishnah and Talmud — is densely *legal*. Thousands of pages of category-by-category adjudication. The very tradition you borrow from spends its life doing what you call "Roman."

**6. Your own sonship language refutes you.**
You want to make this about a Father and His children? Paul uses exactly that imagery — and reaches the *opposite* conclusion you're pushing:

> "Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods. But now that you have come to know God... how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles of the world, whose slaves you want to be once more? **You observe days and months and seasons and years!** I am afraid I may have labored over you in vain." (Gal 4:8–11)

Paul names the symptom of false sonship explicitly: observing days, months, seasons, and years. He didn't call it the Father's loving instruction. He called it a return to slavery — and feared his labor over such people was wasted.

**7. The actual question.**
Strip the vocabulary. The question on the table since Acts 15 is one sentence:

*Are Gentile believers obligated to keep the Mosaic ceremonial code?*

The Jewish apostles, in Hebraic categories, by the Spirit, answered no (Acts 15:28). When someone tried to override that answer in Galatia, Paul called it a different gospel and pronounced anathema (Gal 1:8–9). The book of Hebrews — the most Hebraic letter in the New Testament, written by a Jew, to Jews, from the Hebrew Scriptures — declares the first covenant obsolete and ready to vanish (Heb 8:13).

You can call all of that "Augustine." But Augustine wasn't at the Jerusalem Council. The apostles were. They made your "Roman" distinction before Rome had a single church.

So the choice in front of you isn't "Hebraic vs. Roman." It's whether you'll submit to what the Jewish apostles actually wrote — or keep substituting sentimental vocabulary for the answer they already gave.

— Tudor
